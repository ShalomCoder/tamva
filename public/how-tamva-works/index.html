Decisioning explained

How TAMVA decides on a transaction

Every transaction is judged by a fixed, transparent scorecard — not a black box. TAMVA gathers the facts it already has, runs a checklist of red flags, adds up their weights, and turns that number into one of three actions: approve, review, or block.

0The idea in one paragraph

A rules engine, not a learned model.

Imagine a seasoned fraud analyst with a laminated scorecard. They look at the transaction, look at what they already know about the customer, and tick off any red flags they see. Each flag carries a fixed number of points. When they're done, they add up the points and read off a verdict. The whole process is written down, the same for every transaction, and can be replayed and explained line by line.

There is no training step, no probability, and no hidden weights. TAMVA doesn't "learn" from data at runtime — it only recalls history (how much money moved, how often, over what period) and compares it against thresholds that a human chose. That is what "without an AI or ML model" means here: the intelligence lives entirely in expert-authored rules and thresholds.

Same inputs, same answer, every time. A given evaluation always produces the same score and the same action, and it can always list exactly which flags fired and how much each contributed. That property — determinism plus explainability — is the entire point.

1Gather the facts

Before judging, TAMVA assembles a small "feature snapshot" about the moment.

About the transaction

  • The amount and its currency.
  • The channel (how it came in) and time of day.
  • The account it concerns and who it's for.

About the customer's history

  • The current account balance and how old the account is.
  • How many transactions happened in the last 24 hours and 7 days.
  • Total money in / money out over 90 days.
  • Whether there is any history at all ("thin file" detection).

All of the historical figures come from querying the ledger and transaction records that are already stored. This is the only sense in which TAMVA has "memory" — it is a database lookup, not a model.

2Run the red-flag checklist

Every flag is a plain comparison. If it's true, it adds a fixed weight.

The default engine ships with 11 rules across six categories. Each rule has a severity, and severity determines the points it contributes: LOW = 10 MEDIUM = 30 HIGH = 60 CRITICAL = 100.

Behavioural Atypical versus 90-day inflows amount > 2× 90-day inflow HIGH · 60

Data quality Thin ledger history no transaction history for the customer LOW · 10
Flags are independent and cumulative. A single serious flag may not be enough on its own — it's the combination that escalates a transaction, which is exactly how scorecards have worked in banking for decades.

3Add up the score

Sum the weights of every flag that fired, then read the score band.

The total is capped at 1,000 so the scale stays bounded. The resulting number is compared against four bands calibrated to the checklist's reachable range:

0 – 59   LOWcalm
60 – 140   MEDIUMwatch
141 – 250   HIGHelevated
251 +   CRITICALsevere

4Choose the action

The band maps to one of three decisions.

LOW / MEDIUM

Approve

Allow the transaction to proceed. No human intervention.

HIGH

Review

Hold for a human analyst, who sees the exact flags and score.

CRITICAL

Block

Reject the transaction outright.

When a transaction lands in Review or Block, TAMVA also opens a case automatically — with the score, the level, and the list of reason codes attached as evidence — so nothing that needs attention falls through the cracks. Every evaluation, the feature snapshot it used, and each individual signal are written down in a single all-or-nothing transaction, and an event is published so other systems can react.

Why do it without AI or ML?

The properties that matter in financial risk are transparency and repeatability.

Explainable by construction

Every decision can point to the exact flags and weights behind it. There's no "the model said so."

Deterministic

Same inputs always yield the same score and action. No randomness, no retraining drift.

Auditable

Rules, thresholds, ruleset version and policy version are recorded with each evaluation, so past decisions can be reconstructed.

Tunable by people, not pipelines

To change behaviour you adjust a threshold or a weight — no data science, no GPU, no retraining cycle.

This is also why the engine records which ruleset, model version and decision policy produced a result. Today those point at the built-in baseline. The same interface is designed so a financial institution can later supply its own ruleset or policy — and, if it ever wants to, place a statistical or machine-learned model behind the exact same, still-explainable contract.

Trade-offs to keep in mind

  • It only knows what someone thought to encode. If no rule covers a fraud pattern, the engine is blind to it. Coverage improves by adding rules, not by feeding it more data.
  • Thresholds need periodic tuning. Amounts, velocities and ratios drift as a business and its customers change, so weights and bands should be revisited against real outcomes.
  • Simple rules can be gamed. A determined actor who understands the checklist can stay just under a threshold. Layered rules and cumulative scoring raise the cost of doing so.
  • Garbage in, garbage out. A "thin file" (no history) genuinely weakens the signal — which is precisely why the engine flags thin files explicitly rather than silently treating them as safe.
